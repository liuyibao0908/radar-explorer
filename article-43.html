How the Whole Radar Works Together

Ten steps, repeated thousands of times a second.

On this page
01The ten steps 02The loop closes 03Cycle animation 04Where the time goes 05The weakest link 06Radar networks

In the last nine articles, we looked at each part of a radar one by one. Now let's put them together and see how one full detection happens.

One full cycle: transmitter creates the signal → duplexer switches to send → antenna sends the wave out → wave bounces off the target → antenna receives the echo → duplexer switches to receive → receiver amplifies the signal → signal processor picks out the target → display shows it → servo keeps the antenna scanning.

These ten steps repeat over and over, sometimes thousands of times per second. If any step fails, detection suffers. If the transmitter is too weak, the radar can't see far. If the receiver is not sensitive enough, it misses small targets. If the processor is too slow, it can't track fast targets.

Think of a radar like a sports team: every position does its job to score. A radar is not one amazing part — it is the whole system working together.

01 THE TEN STEPS

One Cycle, Part by Part

Five steps send the wave out, five bring it back and turn it into a mark on a screen.

02 THE LOOP CLOSES

Why It Has to Loop

One measurement gives you one direction. You need a whole circle to know where anything is.

Pulse repetition frequency=600 pulses per second
Measurements per sweep=600 × 0.04 s = 24 pulses per degree
Pulses needed to integrate=32 — so 12 pulses per degree
Result=360° covered in 0.03 s → 20 sweeps per second

The loop exists because a target is only well measured after enough pulses have hit it from the same direction.

03 ANIMATION: THE CYCLE

Watching Ten Steps Run

The whole loop, compressed. Watch which block lights up at each stage.

1-3. TRANSMIT -- power, switch, focused beam

4-5. OUT AND BACK -- the wave leaves and returns

6-8. RECEIVE -- switch, amplify, listen

9-10. DECIDE -- separate the target, draw the pixel

The cycle is identical every time. Only the bearing changes, and that change comes from the servo.

04 WHERE THE TIME GOES

One Round Trip, Broken Down

A radar at 100 km has 66 milliseconds of physics and 200 nanoseconds of electronics.

Wave travels 100 km out=333 μs
Wave travels 100 km back=333 μs
Total round trip=666 μs → 100 km in 667 μs
Duplexer switching=1–5 μs
Receiver + processing=1–10 μs
Servo moves on=the remaining 1.7 ms of the PRF

This is why every microsecond of electronics matters: the wave is out of your control for 666 of the 700 microseconds.

05 THE WEAKEST LINK

Where the Budget Goes Wrong

Improve one part and the limit simply moves somewhere else.

TX

Not Enough Power

Range grows as the fourth root of power. Quadrupling the transmitter buys only about 1.4 times the distance — a very expensive improvement.

RX

Not Sensitive Enough

A receiver that is 3 dB worse than it should be loses real detection range outright. Unlike power, this is not recoverable later.

PROC

Too Slow

If processing lags behind the sweep, the display shows a picture of where things were, not where they are. Track prediction papers over the gap.

SERVO

Too Slow to Point

A faster transmitter does nothing if the beam passes a target before the processor has finished with the previous direction.

DUPLEXER

Too Slow to Switch

Recovery time sets a blind zone at close range. Everything inside it is simply not observed, no matter how good the rest of the radar is.

TEAM

The Real Answer

Designs are limited by the tightest part, so engineers balance them rather than maximising any one. A balanced radar beats a brilliant one.

The chain has no weakest link in the usual sense — it has a limiting link, and moving it requires redesigning everything around it.

06 RADAR NETWORKS

Many Radars, One Picture

Two radars cover the gaps the other one cannot see.

COVERAGE

Filling the Gaps

A target hidden by terrain from one site is often visible from another. Several sites together see continuously where one site sees in patches.

DOPPLER

Two Look Rates

A slow radar sees far but measures speed poorly; a fast one is the reverse. A network combining both gets long range and accurate speed at once.

STEALTH

Different Aspects

A shape designed to be invisible from the front is usually visible from the side. Sites at different angles cover each other's blind aspects.

TRACK

Shared Tracks

Each radar passes its detections to a fusion centre, which correlates them into one track. Two confirmations from different sites are worth far more than two from one.

COST

The Price

Several installations, several sets of buildings, and a communications network that itself needs protecting. Networks buy coverage with capital and complexity.

SCALE

The Two Extremes

A chain of cheap short-range radars covering one building, and a national network of long-range radars. Same ten steps, thirty times over.

Networking does not change the physics of any single radar. It changes what the operator sees by combining many incomplete pictures into one.

ONE-LINE SUMMARY

A radar works only when all its parts work together.
