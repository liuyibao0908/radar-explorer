The Transmitter — The Radar's Voice

Shout, pause, listen. The pause is the whole trick.

On this page

A radar has to "shout" to hear an echo. The transmitter is its voice.

The transmitter creates high-frequency radio waves. More power means longer range. But power can't be infinite — it uses electricity, creates heat, and takes up space. Think of shouting in a valley: a louder voice travels farther, but it tires you out.

A pulse radar shouts, pauses, and listens — it doesn't shout non-stop. Shorter pulses give better range resolution; longer pulses carry more energy. Common devices include magnetrons, klystrons, traveling wave tubes, and solid-state amplifiers.

The transmitter also has to work with the receiver without interfering with itself. Its efficiency and stability directly affect radar performance.

01 WHY POWER IS HARD

The Valley Metaphor

More power buys range, and charges you in electricity, heat and volume.

02 SHOUT, PAUSE, LISTEN

Why a Radar Stops Shouting

If it shouted continuously it would drown out the very echo it is waiting for.

03 ANIMATION: PULSE WIDTH

Short Pulse or Long Pulse

Two targets walk toward the radar. See what the pulse length does to them.

1. LONG PULSE -- strong echo, two targets blur into one

2. SHORT PULSE -- weaker, but the two targets separate

3. RESOLUTION -- spacing sets the shortest gap you can see

4. TRADE-OFF -- energy and clarity pull in opposite directions

Range resolution is roughly half the pulse length. A 1 μs pulse can separate targets 150 m apart; a 10 μs pulse needs 1.5 km.

04 WORKED EXAMPLE

Two Pulses, Two Radars

Same target range, same band. Only the pulse length differs.

Pulse A length=1 μs
Pulse A resolution=150 m — sees two aircraft separately
Pulse B length=10 μs
Pulse B resolution=1.5 km — they merge into one blob

Long pulses are for weather (where features are kilometres wide). Short pulses are for air traffic (where aircraft are hundreds of metres).

05 THE DEVICES

What Actually Makes the Power

Four technologies, in the order they appeared.

1940s

Magnetron

A tube where electrons spiral through a magnetic field and excite a resonant cavity. Cheap, rugged, still the standard for weather radar peak power.

1950s

Klystron

Electrons pass through a series of cavities, first bunched then decelerated. Amplifies an input signal instead of generating it. Very high gain, very expensive.

1960s

Traveling Wave Tube

An electron beam travels along a helix slower than light, so it keeps interacting and growing. Broad bandwidth, mostly used on radar and satellite ground stations.

1970s

Solid-State Amplifier

Many transistor modules combined in parallel. No vacuum, no warm-up, long life — but power per module is limited, so hundreds are needed for megawatts.

GaN

Modern Modules

Gallium nitride switches handle far more power than silicon at radio frequencies. This is what makes compact solid-state weather radars practical today.

EFFICIENCY

Why It Matters

A magnetron runs near 10 percent efficiency. That means for every 100 kW of RF output it draws about 1 MW from the grid, and dumps the rest as heat.

Tubes generate, amplifiers grow. The modern trend is thousands of tiny solid-state modules replacing one big tube.

06 SELF-INTERFERENCE

How to Not Hear Yourself

The transmitter is the loudest thing in the building. The receiver must survive it.

ONE-LINE SUMMARY

The transmitter is the radar's voice — it sends the signal out.
