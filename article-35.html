What Does a Radar Look Like?

One diagram, eight parts, one complete cycle.

On this page
01The parts list 02The radar body 03One full cycle 04Worked example 05Why split it up? 06Where these live

Have you ever seen a radar? That spinning "dish" on top of an airport, a ship, or a weather station — that's a radar. But a radar is not just one antenna. It is a whole team of machines.

A radar has these main parts: antenna, transmitter, receiver, duplexer, signal processor, display, servo system, and power supply. Think of it like a person: the antenna is the eyes and mouth, the transmitter is the voice, the receiver is the ears, the signal processor is the brain, the display is the face, the servo is the neck, and power and cooling are the backbone.

Why split it up like this? Because a radar does something very complex: it sends out waves, receives them back, finds targets in the noise, and shows the result in a way people can understand. Each part handles one step, and none can be missing.

One full cycle: send out, receive back, calculate, and show. It sounds simple, but every step involves precise timing and high-speed processing. Radars come in many sizes — some as small as a box, some as tall as a building — but the basic structure is the same.

03 ANIMATION: ONE FULL CYCLE

Watching a Pulse Go Out and Come Back

The animation sends a pulse to a target, brings the echo back, filters it, and paints the result.

1. TRANSMIT -- the transmitter sends a short pulse

2. REFLECT -- the pulse bounces off the target

3. PROCESS -- the processor pulls the target out of the noise

4. DISPLAY -- the console paints the result

Send, receive, calculate, show. The whole loop takes milliseconds; only the antenna turn takes seconds.

04 WORKED EXAMPLE

Which Part Does What?

Match each step of one cycle to the part that does it.

Produce the pulse=Transmitter
Send it into space=Antenna
Hear it come back=Receiver
Separate out and in=Duplexer
Find it in the noise=Signal processor
Show it to a human=Display

Six steps, six parts. Change one and the chain breaks.

05 THE HUMAN ANALOGY

Radar as a Person

The easiest way to remember eight parts is to borrow a body.

ANTENNA

Eyes and Mouth

The dish radiates the outgoing wave and catches the incoming echo. Same surface, two jobs.

TX / RX

Voice and Ears

The transmitter shouts, the receiver listens. The receiver is far more sensitive than the transmitter is loud.

PROCESSOR

The Brain

Thousands of pulses arrive at once, drowned in noise. The processor runs an FFT per pulse and decides what is a target.

DISPLAY

The Face

Raw numbers are useless to a human. The display turns them into a picture you can read in a second.

SERVO

The Neck

The servo turns the dish so it points where the operator wants. With a phased array the neck barely moves.

POWER

The Backbone

A big weather radar draws as much power as a small town. Cooling is not optional; it is part of the design.

Dish, throat, ears, brain, face, neck, backbone. Once you can picture it, the block diagram is obvious.

06 WHERE THESE LIVE

Same Parts, Different Sizes

From a box on a drone to a building on a hill, the block list never changes.

AIRPORT

Approach Radar

A rotating dish about 3 m across, on a small tower. Short range, high update rate, tracks aircraft on final approach.

MARINE

Naval Surface Search

Slower rotation, very long range, built to survive salt and vibration. Often several radars stacked on one mast.

WEATHER

WSR-88D / PAR

A radome about 25 m across on a hardened tower. The whole structure is the shelter; electronics sit inside it.

SPACE

Synthetic Aperture

No dish at all. A moving antenna records successive pulses and the processor does the focusing after the fact.

GROUND

GPR and Through-Wall

Small enough to carry. Same eight blocks, but the transmitter is a pistol and the display is a laptop screen.

SMALLEST

Millimetre-Wave Modules

On a chip, the antenna and the power stage share the same few square millimetres. Range drops to tens of metres.

Size changes, structure does not. That is why one diagram can describe a hundred different radars.

ONE-LINE SUMMARY

A radar is a whole team of machines working together, not just one big antenna.
