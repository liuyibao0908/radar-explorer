The Antenna — Hands and Ears

The reflector is the difference between a bulb and a flashlight.

On this page

The antenna is the most visible part of a radar. Some look like dishes, some like flat panels, some like walls. What does it actually do?

The antenna does two jobs: when sending, it focuses radio waves in one direction; when receiving, it catches the weak waves coming back. Think of a flashlight: a plain bulb shines everywhere, but a flashlight has a reflector that sends light far and focused.

A bigger antenna means a narrower beam and better accuracy, but a smaller search area. Different shapes serve different uses: parabolic, flat panel, array. Some antennas spin — that's mechanical scanning. Some don't — that's electronic scanning.

Antennas also have parameters like polarization, gain, and side lobes. Side lobes are like light leaking from a flashlight — they cause interference and clutter. The antenna is the radar's first gate, and it directly affects detection.

01 TWO JOBS

Hands and Ears

The same metal surface radiates on transmit and collects on receive. Reciprocity is the whole trick.

02 BULB VS FLASHLIGHT

Why a Reflector Changes Everything

A bare radiator wastes almost all its power. The reflector recovers it.

03 ANIMATION: APERTURE VS BEAM

Watch the Beam Narrow

Grow the dish and the beam gets thinner. Thin beam means better angle accuracy and a slower sweep.

1. SMALL DISH -- wide beam, weak and blunt

2. MEDIUM DISH -- beam tightens, gain rises

3. LARGE DISH -- narrow beam, sharp edges

4. TRADE-OFF -- precision up, scan speed down

Beam width and aperture trade against each other. That is the central design choice in every antenna.

04 WORKED EXAMPLE

Trade the Trade-Off

Two radars, same band, different dishes. Here is what each one is good at.

Radar A aperture=3 m — airport approach
Radar A beam=wide, updates every 1 s
Radar B aperture=25 m — weather radar
Radar B beam=narrow, full sweep in 4 min

Airport radars want fresh data; weather radars want sharp angles. Different dishes, same physics.

05 SHAPES AND SCANNING

Dish, Panel, Array

Three common shapes, three scanning styles, six words worth knowing.

PARABOLIC

The Classic Dish

A curved surface with one feed. Excellent gain, simple to build, high wind load. Needs a motor to turn.

ARRAY

The Phased Array

Hundreds of small radiators, each with its own phase shifter. Steers the beam in microseconds without moving anything.

FLAT PANEL

The Wall

Low profile, low wind load, installable on rooftops and masts. Usually a small array with fixed electronic steering.

MECHANICAL

It Turns

One beam, one direction at a time. The sweep rate is set by how fast the motor can turn the whole structure.

ELECTRONIC

It Doesn't

Many beams at once. The radar can dwell on a storm for a second, then jump 30 degrees, without any moving parts.

HYBRID

Best of Both

Today’s weather radars turn slowly just to keep the antenna facing the weather, while the array does the actual scanning.

Shape decides the beam; the scanning method decides how fast you can move that beam.

06 SIDE LOBES

The Light That Leaks Out

No antenna is perfect. Some energy always escapes sideways, and that energy comes back as clutter.

ONE-LINE SUMMARY

The antenna is the radar's hands and ears — it sends and receives waves.
