Why Some Antennas Spin and Others Don't

Two ways to point a beam. One costs a motor; the other costs a computer.

On this page

Some radar antennas spin all the time. Others never move. Why?

Spinning ones: mechanical-scan radar, which turns the antenna to change beam direction. Still ones: phased-array radar, which steers the beam electronically. Mechanical scanning is simple and cheap, but slow. Phased array is fast and flexible, but complex and expensive.

Mechanical scanning is like an oscillating fan; phased array is like many small lights working together. Phased array can track many targets at once; mechanical scanning looks at one direction at a time. Modern warships and fighter jets use phased array more and more.

But mechanical-scan radar still has its own uses and advantages.

01 THE TWO ARCHITECTURES

One Beam, Two Ways to Move It

In both cases there is exactly one beam. The difference is what carries it across the sky.

02 DWELL TIME IS THE DIFFERENCE

One Number Explains Almost Everything

How long the beam rests on one direction decides what the radar can know.

A 1° beam on a 36°/s sweep=28 ms per direction
Pulses you can spend there=about 16 at 600 Hz
Needed to integrate=32 pulses — so the scan must slow
A phased array at 1 ms=600 pulses in the same 1 ms
It can dwell on three=targets at once and still integrate fully
Then jump to a fourth=in the time the dish has not finished 2°

This is the whole argument in one line: the array does not scan faster, it does many things at once. The dish can only ever be looking at one thing.

03 ANIMATION: FAN VERSUS LIGHTS

The Oscillating Fan and the Panel of Lights

Same job, same sky. Watch how each one covers it.

1. DISH -- one beam, sweeping, never in two places at once

2. ARRAY -- one beam, jumping instantly to a new bearing

3. THREE TARGETS -- the array holds all three simultaneously

4. THE SAME THREE -- the dish reaches them one at a time

Neither is better at pointing. The difference is that a point of light stays lit while you look at something else.

04 WORKED EXAMPLE

Missile Defence and Air Traffic Control

The same physics, sized for two very different jobs.

ATC wants=fresh data on 20 aircraft within 60 km
Missile defence wants=many high-speed targets, guided continuously
A dish at 60 km=updates every 1–2 s — fine for the first, useless for the second
A rotating array=updates every 0.1 s — the reason arrays took over this role
A fixed array=10 Hz or better, and it can hold several interceptors at once
Consequence=every modern fighter radar is a fixed array

A fighter cannot rely on a radar that looks away for two seconds while it is closing on a missile. That single requirement decided the design.

05 THE TRADE-OFFS

What Each One Is Bad At

A fair comparison has to name both sides' weaknesses.

DISH SLOW

One Direction at a Time

A fast, close target can cross the beam between two visits. The radar knows it was there once, and not where it went.

DISH SLOW

No Elevation Without Tilting

A single-axis dish covers only one elevation band per pass. Pointing up means nothing is being watched below.

DISH SLOW

Wear and Storm Loading

A bearing takes wear, ice loads the radome, and a gust pushes the beam off. A 25 m dish needs a real maintenance regime.

ARRAY BAD

Cost and Heat

Thousands of modules, each needing power and cooling. The support building often costs more than the antenna face.

ARRAY BAD

Scan Angle Is Not Free

A flat face steers poorly far off axis, so a real system still mounts the array on a slow-drive pedestal to face the working hemisphere.

ARRAY BAD

Failure Adds Up

Losing one element degrades the beam slightly; losing a whole subarray leaves a notch in it. Faults have to be found and worked around.

Neither architecture wins on merit. Each one is bad at the thing the other one is good at, which is exactly why both are still in service.

06 WHY THE DISH SURVIVES

Cases Where Mechanical Still Wins

A rotating antenna is not a legacy technology. It is the right answer to several questions.

COST

Long Range, Low Budget

Range needs aperture and power, not electronics. A big dish on open ground covers 400 km for a fraction of an array's cost.

BEAM

High Gain per Watt

A parabolic dish is inherently efficient, with no phase-shifter losses. For a fixed long-range warning role, that is decisive.

SECTOR

A Deliberate Gap

Sometimes you want a radar that is blind over your own city. A rotating dish is naturally blanked in a fixed sector.

WIDEBAND

Several Wavelengths

A dish works across a broad band, so it can be switched between frequencies for different jobs. An array's modules are built for one band.

REPAIR

Simple to Understand

When a rotating radar stops, the cause is usually mechanical and visible. Array diagnostics are genuinely harder in the field.

BOTH

The Hybrid

Many modern weather radars are arrays on slow pedestals: electronic beam steering for the weather, with a servo keeping the face pointed at the storms.

The spinning dish is not obsolete. It is simply no longer the only sensible answer, and it never will be.

ONE-LINE SUMMARY

Whether it spins depends on mechanical or electronic scanning.
