The Receiver — Finding Echoes in the Noise

Hearing one voice across a crowded playground.

On this page
01How weak is weak 02The signal chain 03Noise vs signal 04Worked example 05Dynamic range 06Stealth detection

The wave a radar sends out comes back very, very weak after bouncing off a target. How can it still be heard?

The receiver's job: amplify the weak echo and pick it out from the noise. Think of hearing a friend call your name across a noisy playground. The more sensitive the receiver, the better it finds small or distant targets.

Too much sensitivity also amplifies noise, so filtering is needed. The receiver also handles dynamic range: strong signals must not saturate, weak signals must not be lost. Common parts include low-noise amplifiers, mixers, intermediate-frequency amplifiers, and detectors.

When one antenna both sends and receives, a duplexer protects the receiver. Receiver performance directly affects whether a radar can detect stealth targets.

01 HOW WEAK IS WEAK

Four Round Trips of Loss

The echo crosses the same distance twice, and spreads its energy over a sphere.

02 THE SIGNAL CHAIN

Four Boxes That Save the Signal

Each stage adds gain, but the first one decides how much noise comes along.

03 ANIMATION: SIGNAL VS NOISE

Amplify, Then Filter

Turn the gain up too far and the target drowns along with everything else.

1. INCOMING -- one weak echo buried in noise

2. AMPLIFY -- everything grows, signal and noise alike

3. FILTER -- the noise floor drops away

4. CLEAN -- a clear pulse you can actually use

Amplification raises signal and noise together. Only filtering separates them — that is why a receiver is far more than a bigger amp.

04 WORKED EXAMPLE

Putting the Numbers Together

One stealth aircraft, one radar, and the arithmetic that decides detection.

Transmit power=100 kW peak
Range=100 km
Radar cross section=0.01 m² (stealth-shaped)
Received power=about −140 dBm
Thermal noise there=about −128 dBm
Signal below noise=integration over thousands of pulses recovers it

A single pulse is invisible. Averaging thousands of them raises the target above the noise without raising the noise floor.

05 DYNAMIC RANGE

Loud and Quiet at Once

A ground return and a distant bird arrive at the same input, differing by 100 dB.

STC

Sensitivity Time Control

Turn the gain up only while listening to echoes, after the transmit pulse ends. Close returns are quiet, far returns are loud — the gain rises with time.

AGC

Automatic Gain Control

A feedback loop measures the average output and adjusts the gain. It keeps a storm from pinning the display while a weak target still gets recorded.

LOG

Logarithmic Amplifier

A 100 dB span is far too much for a linear display. Logarithmic compression fits the whole range on one screen by turning dB into millimetres.

NOISE FIGURE

How Much You Added

Noise figure measures the extra noise an amplifier contributes. A low-noise amplifier has a figure near 1 dB; a cheap wideband one might be 6 dB, which is a real detection loss.

FILTER

Matched Filtering

The filter is shaped to the pulse the transmitter actually sent. This maximises the signal-to-noise ratio at the instant the echo arrives.

INTEGRATE

Many Pulses, One Target

A real target stays put in range and azimuth across many pulses; noise does not. Summing N pulses raises the signal as N but the noise only as the square root of N.

STC, AGC and integration are the three ways a real receiver copes with a 100 dB spread of return strengths.

06 STEALTH DETECTION

The Receiver Is Where Stealth Dies

A stealth aircraft does not defeat the wave. It just gives the receiver almost nothing to work with.

LONG WAVE

Low Frequency

Longer wavelengths need a smaller aircraft to be stealthy. VHF and UHF radars therefore see stealth shapes more easily than X-band does.

PASSIVE

Listening Only

A passive radar emits nothing, so it is not a target itself. It picks up the energy the aircraft already reflects from other transmitters.

INTEGRATION

Long Dwell

Stealth relies on being small in a single pulse. Dwelling on one spot for thousands of pulses brings the signal back above the noise floor.

NETWORK

More Eyes

No single radar sees it, but four receivers sharing detections often do. A stealth shape that hides from one angle is usually visible from another.

IR / EO

Other Sensors

Infrared and electro-optical sensors ignore radar cross section entirely. They trade weather independence for a very different view of the sky.

CONCLUSION

Not a Force Field

Stealth reduces detection range; it does not make an aircraft invisible. Better receiver processing closes part of that gap every decade.

A radar that cannot hear a whisper still finds it by listening longer, at a longer wavelength, with more receivers.

ONE-LINE SUMMARY

The receiver finds echoes in the noise — the more sensitive, the better.
