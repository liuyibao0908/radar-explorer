Power and Cooling — The Radar's Backbone

The part nobody photographs, and the part that decides whether it runs at all.

On this page
01Where the power goes 02The heat budget 03Cooling loop animation 04Worked example 05Why it gets big 06When the grid fails

A radar uses a lot of power and creates a lot of heat. How does it keep going?

Power supply: feeds the transmitter, receiver, signal processor, and servo. Cooling: air or liquid cooling to stop parts from overheating. Think of a gaming PC — you need enough power and good cooling, or it lags and shuts down.

In high-power radars, power and cooling systems take up a lot of space. Power must be stable — voltage swings affect performance. Cooling must be reliable — overheating burns out parts.

Some radars also have generators and UPS systems to avoid power cuts. If the backbone fails, even the best radar can't perform.

01 WHERE THE POWER GOES

Only a Tenth Comes Out as Radio

A radar draws far more from the grid than it puts into the air. The rest becomes heat.

02 THE HEAT BUDGET

Removing a Megawatt of Heat

Heat has to go somewhere. The choice of where is the whole design.

AIR

Forced Air

Fans move air through filters and heat exchangers. Simple, cheap and easy to maintain — but air carries far less heat per cubic metre than water, so it needs a lot of moving volume.

WATER

Liquid Loop

Water carries about 4000 times more heat per unit volume than air. Most high-power installations use a water loop even though the final heat rejection is still often to air.

SEALED

Closed Loop

The coolant never leaves the building; external radiators dump the heat to the atmosphere. This is what a modern weather radar does inside its radome.

COLD

Why It Is Cold Outside

A radome is white for a reason. Sunlight on a dark dome of that size would add tens of kilowatts of heat on a still, clear day.

LIMIT

Summer Peak

Cooling is sized for the hottest day of the year, not the average. On a hot still afternoon there is less margin than at any other time.

BLOWN

When It Fails

A coolant pump stopping takes the radar down within minutes. Most stations watch coolant temperature as closely as they watch the transmitter.

Water in, air out. The loop exists because air is a poor conductor of heat and a radome is a very large surface to cool.

03 ANIMATION: THE COOLING LOOP

Watching Heat Leave the Building

Follow a packet of heat from the transmitter to the outside air.

1. GENERATED -- the transmitter makes heat, not just waves

2. ABSORBED -- coolant picks it up at the cold plate

3. CARRIED -- a pump moves it through the loop

4. REJECTED -- radiators pass it into the open air

A closed loop does not consume water, but it does depend on pumps running. Every stage is a possible failure.

04 WORKED EXAMPLE

One Radar's Power Bill

A typical high-power weather radar, and where every kilowatt goes.

Transmitter, peak=800 kW
Duty cycle=0.2% — it is on for 2 ms, off for 1 s
Transmitter, average=1.6 kW
Electronics + servo=40 kW continuous
Cooling and losses=25 kW continuous
Site total=about 65 kW → 570 MWh a year

The peak is what the cooling must handle instantly. The average is what the electricity bill looks like. They differ by a factor of two thousand.

05 WHY IT GETS BIG

Quality Has a Physical Size

Nothing in a radar can be made perfect, because perfection would need infinite mass.

UPS

Ride-Through

A radar cannot be stopped and restarted casually; the tube warms up over minutes and the crew lose the picture. Batteries hold the site for roughly ten minutes.

GEN

Generator

A diesel set on site takes over when the grid fails. It must start, fuel and run unattended for hours, and it is tested on a schedule for that reason.

REG

Voltage Regulation

A magnetron's output frequency and power depend on its anode voltage. Grid sag of a few per cent becomes a measurable shift in the transmitted wave.

TRANSIENT

Switching Surges

Striking a magnetron draws a very short, very large pulse of current. Supply gear is sized for that surge, not for the average load.

GROUND

Earthing

The radome is on a concrete foundation with its own earth. It protects the structure, and it keeps the RF ground plane consistent.

SPACE

The Hidden Half

In a high-power radar, the support building is often larger than the tower. It holds the feeder, the cooling plant, the generator and the spares.

The most advanced part of a radar is often not in the tower at all. It is in the hut beside it.

06 WHEN THE GRID FAILS

The Priority Order

Not everything can keep running. Something has to be chosen.

T+0

Grid Drops

The switchgear transfers to the generator or the UPS within seconds. The transmitter is the first thing switched off, because it is the biggest load and the least needed for a moment.

T+1min

Survival Mode

On battery or generator power the radar reduces to the receiver, processor and display. No transmission, but the site still sees whatever is already in the air.

T+10min

Shutdown

If the outage continues the site shuts down in a controlled order, cooling stopped last so the electronics can cool down as they go.

SAFE

Why This Order

A warm magnetron is far harder to restart than a cold one, but damaging it is worse than losing coverage. The sequence is always built around not breaking hardware.

SITE

Remote Sites

Offshore or mountain stations may have no grid at all, so a generator is the normal supply and a large battery bridges the start delay.

COST

The Expensive Part

In many procurements the power, cooling and shelter cost more than the radar itself. That ratio is not widely known outside the industry.

The backbone is invisible during normal operation, which is exactly why it is easy to under-budget.

ONE-LINE SUMMARY

Power and cooling are the radar's backbone — they keep it running.
