The Display — What Are Those Dots?

Position is distance and bearing. Brightness is strength. Everything else is an overlay.

On this page

What do those dots and lines on a radar screen really mean?

The display turns processed signals into something people can read. A-scope: distance on the horizontal axis, signal strength on the vertical. PPI: like a map, with you at the center and targets around you.

A dot's position shows distance and direction; its brightness shows echo strength. Think of a mini-map in a video game — you at the center, bright dots are enemies. Modern radars often use color displays, where different colors mean different strengths.

The display also overlays maps, tracks, and warnings. Operators use it to judge targets and make decisions.

01 TWO WAYS TO PLOT

A-Scope vs PPI

The same data, two very different pictures. One answers "how far", the other answers "where".

02 READING A PPI

Polar to Cartesian

A dot is stored as one number for distance and one for angle. The screen converts them to a position.

03 ANIMATION: THE SWEEP

Watch the Sweep Paint the Picture

A PPI builds one picture out of thousands of individual measurements, one per pulse.

1. ONE PULSE -- a single line, one direction only

2. MANY PULSES -- rotate, and blips appear at each target

3. PAINTED -- the accumulated picture builds up

4. FADING -- old echoes decay so the display stays current

Nothing is stored as a picture. Each blip is a single measurement, placed by its own range and bearing, and the picture is an illusion of accumulation.

04 WORKED EXAMPLE

Reading One Blip Out Loud

An operator sees this and says one sentence. Here is every step behind it.

Where on screen=upper right, third ring out
Converting to numbers=42 km, bearing 037°
Brightness says=large — a metal aircraft, not a bird
Track behaviour=moving 240 m/s, inbound, closing
Decision=vector the interceptor, arm the ranges

A display is a decision instrument, not a picture. Every pixel exists because somebody might have to act on it.

05 COLOUR AND BRIGHTNESS

Encoding Strength

Echo strength spans 100 dB. The screen has to compress that into something an eye can rank.

MONO

Black and Green

The original phosphor display. Brightness alone carried the strength, and green was the easiest colour for a long-persistence tube. Still used where colour is unavailable.

COLOUR

False Colour

A rainbow ramp where hue means strength. Far easier to rank at a glance than brightness, but it wastes a channel — most displays keep one colour reserved for alarms.

ALARM

Reserved Red

Red is removed from the strength ramp and used only for a priority target or a warning. If everything can be red, nothing is.

GRAIN

Speckle

Below a threshold, a blip is shown as speckle rather than a solid mark. Speckle is usually noise or clutter; a solid return has persisted across several pulses.

VIDEO

Integration

Enlarging each pixel to cover several range-azimuth cells averages neighbouring noise. The picture gets smoother and weak targets survive, at the cost of resolution.

HISTOGRAM

Side Panel

Because position is now in polar coordinates, vertical stripes are clutter, lines are interference and isolated dots are noise. The side panel turns those into a shape you can recognise.

Colour is a ranking device. The operator's job is to spot which blip is in the wrong place on the scale.

06 LAYERS ON TOP

Everything Except the Radar

Most of what is drawn is not raw echo at all.

COAST

The Shoreline

Drawn from a chart or satellite image. It gives every other mark a fixed reference, which is how an operator knows whether a target is over water or inland.

TRACK

The History

Past positions of the same target joined by a line, with a short line showing its velocity. This is drawn by the tracker, not the radar.

LEAD

The Prediction

A marker extrapolating where the track will be in a chosen time. This is the single most important symbol on the screen and it is pure arithmetic.

VECTORS

Assigned Targets

A line from a friendly unit to its assigned target, or from a missile to the point it is predicted to intercept. Most dots acquire a vector only after a decision is made.

TACGRID

The Tactical Grid

A coordinate grid laid over the picture so a position can be reported as a grid reference rather than a pixel. The bridge between what is seen and what can be said on a radio.

SCAN

The Sweep Itself

A rotating line showing where the antenna points right now. Data outside the sector is stale, and an operator must know which parts of the picture are fresh.

The raw radar picture is a minority of the pixels. The rest is chart data, arithmetic and decisions.

ONE-LINE SUMMARY

The display turns radar signals into readable dots on a screen.
