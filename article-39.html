The Duplexer — How One Antenna Does Both

A door that opens and closes a hundred times a second.

On this page
01The problem 02The door analogy 03Switching animation 04Worked example 05Two technologies 06The alternative

A radar often uses just one antenna for both sending and receiving. How do they not clash?

When sending, the power is very high — it would burn out the receiver. The duplexer protects the receiver during sending, and connects the antenna to the receiver during receiving. Think of a door: it closes the receiver side while sending, and opens it while receiving.

Common types include gas discharge tubes and PIN diode switches. The switch must be fast, or it will miss close-range echoes. It also has to handle high power without breaking itself.

Some radars use separate antennas for sending and receiving, so no duplexer is needed. It is a small but critical part.

01 THE PROBLEM

Two Numbers That Cannot Both Be True

The same wire carries a megawatt out and a trillionth of a watt back.

02 THE DOOR ANALOGY

A Door, Not a Wall

It does not block the signal permanently — it switches, thousands of times a second.

03 ANIMATION: THE SWITCH CYCLE

One Full Pulse Cycle

Watch the door open and close, and see how long the receiver stays deaf.

1. TRANSMIT -- the RX door is shut, power goes out

2. SWITCH -- the device changes state in microseconds

3. LISTEN -- the RX door opens and the receiver recovers

4. DEAF ZONE -- anything closer than recovery time is lost

Recovery time sets the minimum detectable range. A weather radar with 1 μs recovery simply cannot see the first 150 metres.

04 WORKED EXAMPLE

What Recovery Time Costs You

Two radars with the same 300 km range, and very different blind spots.

Speed of light=300 000 km per second
Radar A recovery=1 μs — blind to the first 150 m
Radar B recovery=5 μs — blind to the first 750 m
Round trip=150 km out and back = 1 ms

Recovery is one round trip. A 1 μs device means anything inside 150 metres returns while the switch is still changing state.

05 TWO TECHNOLOGIES

Gas Tube or PIN Diode

Two completely different ways to build the same switch.

GAS

Gas Discharge Tube

A small tube filled with low-pressure gas. In transmit mode the high voltage strikes an arc; the ionised gas becomes conducting and blocks the receiver. It extinguishes when the voltage drops.

PROS

Handles Huge Power

An arc will carry megawatts without complaint, so weather radars with very high peak power have used gas tubes for fifty years. The arc also carries the power the antenna does not radiate.

CONS

Slow and Wearful

Extinction and re-striking take microseconds, and the electrodes erode over time. Only a few hundred pulses per minute are practical.

PIN

PIN Diode

A silicon diode with a wide intrinsic region, used as a switch rather than a rectifier. Forward bias makes it conduct; reverse bias makes it block. No gas, no arc.

PROS

Fast and Reliable

Switching takes nanoseconds and the device has no moving parts or consumable electrodes. Tens of thousands of pulses per second are routine.

CONS

Limited by Current

A single diode carries only tens of amps. Megawatt systems use hundreds in parallel, which is why a phased array can afford them but a classic magnetron radar often could not.

Gas for raw power, silicon for speed. Most modern radars use PIN diodes in large arrays.

06 THE ALTERNATIVE

Skip the Switch Entirely

If two antennas are affordable, the problem does not exist.

BISTATIC

Two Antennas

Transmit and receive on separate antennas far apart. No switching, no isolation problem, and the geometry itself hides the transmitter. Used for missile defence and over-the-horizon radar.

PASSIVE

Receive Only

With no transmitter at all there is nothing to switch. Passive radars listen for other people's energy and are extremely hard to detect themselves.

T/R MODULE

Switch Per Element

In a phased array every element has its own transmit/receive module containing a duplexer. Thousands of small PIN diode switches replace one big fragile one.

CIRCULATOR

The Passive Version

A ferrite circulator routes signals by direction rather than by switching. It needs no control voltage at all, but the isolation is lower and it handles less power.

WHY BOTH

Cost vs Capability

A duplexer is cheap, compact and keeps one antenna pointed at the sky. Bistatic is better physics but far harder to build. Most radars pick the duplexer.

SUMMARY

Small but Critical

Nobody photographs the duplexer, but without it the receiver burns up on the first pulse. It is the least glamorous part of the most expensive machine.

One switch, two antennas, or one PIN diode per array element — every radar solves this trade-off differently.

ONE-LINE SUMMARY

The duplexer is the traffic controller — it keeps sending and receiving from clashing.
