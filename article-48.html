Is Radar Radiation Harmful?

The intensity falls off fast. The rules exist because it is not fast enough.

On this page

Radar uses a lot of power. Is it dangerous to stand next to one?

Radar does send out radio waves, but the strength drops quickly with distance. Proper radars have safety distances and rules. For everyday radar, as long as you don't stay very close for a long time, it's usually safe.

Think of a microwave — don't press your face against it while it's running, but normal use is fine. High-power radar stations set warning zones and keep people away. Whether radiation is harmful depends on frequency, power, distance, and time.

Military and civilian radars both have safety standards. So no need to panic, but do follow the rules.

01 THE FALLOFF PROBLEM

Intensity Falls as One Over R Squared

Spreading a wave over a sphere is why a radar's own neighbourhood is safe but its mast is not.

02 FALLOFF ANIMATION

Walking Away From the Mast

One antenna, one person, and a power level that falls by 100 dB in a short walk.

1. AT THE MAST -- inside the controlled area, restricted

2. WALKING OUT -- the field falls as one over R squared

3. AT THE FENCE -- down 40 dB, far below the limit

4. BEYOND -- the radar is still transmitting, still safe

The radar does not switch off when you leave. It is the distance doing the work.

03 THE MICROWAVE ANALOGY

Why the Analogy Is Reasonable but Wrong

A microwave oven leaks 5 watts into a sealed steel box. A radar runs 100 kilowatts into open air.

SAME

Why It Feels Right

Both are microwave-frequency devices. Both put an intense field inside a small space. Both have a power source you should not open while it runs.

DIFFERENT

The Leak Is Backwards

A microwave oven leaks 5 watts outwards from a small cavity. A radar emits 100 kW into open space, and you are the small cavity standing in it.

SHIELDED

The Oven Is Enclosed

A microwave oven is a Faraday cage. A radar has no cage at all — by design it must radiate into the sky, which is the entire point.

PULSED

Duty Cycle Saves People

Radar transmits in microsecond bursts and listens between them. The time-averaged field is orders of magnitude below the peak, and exposure limits are averaged, not peak.

WHY

Why There Are Fences

Not because a passer-by is in danger, but because someone standing at the mast for a whole shift is. The rules are about occupation, not about passing.

RESULT

The Honest Summary

Radar fields are strong at the antenna and weak at the fence. There is no established mechanism by which a radar causes illness at legal exposure levels.

The microwave comparison is good for intuition and bad for physics. Keep the caution, drop the arithmetic.

04 WORKED EXAMPLE

One Antenna, Four Distances

A 20 kW average-output radar antenna, and the field at a few distances from it.

At the mast, 2 m=well above the occupational limit — fenced off
At 20 m=down 20 dB, around the limit
At the perimeter, 100 m=down 34 dB — this is where the fence is
At 1 km=down 54 dB, far below any limit
At 10 km=down 74 dB — irrelevant to health
The arithmetic behind this=every doubling of distance costs 6 dB

The rule of thumb to remember: double the distance, lose 6 dB. Going from 2 m to 100 m is a factor of 50, which is 34 dB.

05 WHERE THE LIMITS COME FROM

How a Number Is Decided

Exposure limits are not physics. They are the output of a long argument between evidence and precaution.

ICNIRP

The Guideline

International limits for workers and the public, derived from the levels at which established effects appear. Above the limit, thermal effects on tissue are the accepted mechanism.

BODY

SAR

Specific absorption rate, measured in watts per kilogram. Whole-body limits are far lower than local limits, because the whole body cannot shed heat anywhere.

AVERAGE

Time Matters

Limits apply to a time-averaged field. A radar transmitting 0.2 percent of the time is, for exposure purposes, transmitting at a fifth of its power.

BAND

Which Frequency

Radar bands sit in the microwave region, where absorbed energy heats tissue. At lower frequencies absorption is much weaker, at higher it is absorbed in the skin.

SIGN

The Unsettled Part

Long-term effects below the limits are still argued about. The mainstream position is that established evidence supports the limits, while some researchers disagree.

RULE

Why Precaution Persists

Because radar sites are easy to fence off and the cost of doing so is trivial. There is no engineering reason to expose anyone to a limit that can be avoided for free.

Two honest things at once: the limits are conservative, and there is no good reason to test them by standing in the field.

06 WHAT YOU ACTUALLY SEE

Radar in Public Space

Radars sit beside roads, airports and sports grounds. Here is how they handle it.

FENCE

The Perimeter

A physical fence around the mast, sized so that the field at the boundary is comfortably below the public limit. Signs mark it as a controlled area.

RAIL

Height as Shielding

Mounting the antenna high on a mast or tower is the most effective measure available. Height is the cheapest way to buy distance.

PLUME

Waveguide above People

Where power is fed through the air — an airport surface radar, for instance — the waveguide is routed overhead and above head height, not across a walkway.

SHUTDOWN

Switch Off for Work

Maintenance inside the perimeter is done with the transmitter off or at reduced power. The rules are procedural as much as physical.

MONITOR

Measuring It

Field strength is measured at the perimeter as part of routine safety work. The result is recorded, and the fence is sized from that measurement.

BOTTOM

The Practical Answer

Fences, height, shutdown procedures and measurement. None of it implies that standing outside a radar fence is dangerous. It implies that the margin is worth keeping.

A fence around a radar says the field inside is managed, not that the machine is dangerous.

ONE-LINE SUMMARY

Radar has radiation, but following safety distances usually keeps you safe.
